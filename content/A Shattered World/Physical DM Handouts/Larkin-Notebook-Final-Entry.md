# Larkin's Notebook — Final Entry

*Private Journal of Dr. Alistair Larkin, Luminary of the Council of Aerolon. Year 1719.*

---

I have delayed this entry longer than prudence would advise.

The expedition to the Deep, once a matter of academic triumph, has proven instead to be a persistent contaminant of the mind. The psychological residue I brought back from that abyss has now matured into something distinct from ordinary stress or fatigue. It manifests as an antagonistic presence within my cognition. A second temperament, if one wishes to indulge literary metaphor: a Hyde to my Jekyll.

Where once my thoughts were governed by method, analysis, and restraint, I now encounter impulses that advocate abandonment of those principles entirely. It speaks not as an alien intelligence but as a distortion of my own faculties — one that urges violence, cruelty, and sabotage of the very progress to which I have dedicated my life.

I record this not for sympathy, but as documentation. Should future luminaries pursue expeditions into the Deep, I recommend prolonged psychological observation for all returning personnel. My colleagues who survived the journey — many of them leaders of society in their own right — appear thus far unaffected. I pray this remains true.

It would be an unforgivable irony if the very people entrusted with guiding our civilization carried within them the same rot now evident in me.

Progress demands stability in those who lead it.

In preparation for the decision I have now reached, I have taken inventory of my life and the absences within it. My regrets are not numerous in quantity, but they possess weight.

I never married.

I never invested time to the whole of my household.

I never permitted myself the small inefficiencies of ordinary human attachment. Where others sought companionship, I sought discovery. Where others built families, I constructed engines, instruments, and alchemical frameworks.

The exchange was deliberate.

I believed then, and still believe, that the progress of Aerolon justified that exchange.

Yet now, when the mind fractures and the hours lengthen, a quieter voice wonders whether one might have accommodated both.

Such reflections arrive too late to be of practical use.

---

My final obligations concern two individuals whose futures must not be compromised by my deterioration.

**Starglass.**

The girl remains the most remarkable biological phenomenon I have ever encountered. Her physiology continues to demonstrate alchemical plasticity bordering on the impossible. Metallic transformation of tissue remains observable under certain emotional stimuli, suggesting that her body exists in a state somewhere between organism and adaptive alloy.

The hypothesis I leave behind is incomplete but promising.

If her mind can be stabilized within a mechanical lattice — something more resilient than organic neurology — she may survive indefinitely. The ship presently housing her systems may serve as a transitional framework, though its scale is unnecessarily large for long-term habitation.

Two possibilities present themselves:

- **Repatriation to the Deep** — returning her to the environment from which she originated. This option is ethically questionable given the hostility of that region and the apparent indifference of its inhabitants to her absence.
- **Transference** — dismantling the current vessel and constructing a smaller mechanical host capable of sustaining her consciousness.

The second option is technologically viable.

Whether it is morally correct depends upon a single question I have not yet resolved:

*Does she wish to remain here?*

Starglass has always demonstrated a peculiar capacity to perceive my thoughts. If that perception is genuine rather than interpretive intuition, then she will already know that I hope to grant her a life defined by care rather than confinement. She has been rather restless when I write near her recently. I do hope her crude containment has not been too disquieting. Her restlessness only proves my need for haste, and a request for a confidante to properly handle her next steps.

---

**Second only in urgency is Iskander.**

The boy possesses a resolve that Aerolon will one day require. His circumstances have hardened him prematurely, yet they have not extinguished the moral center within him.

He is not aware that I consider him one of the most promising young figures our nation has produced in decades.

If my absence leaves him directionless, I have taken modest steps to ensure he will not be alone. The orphans he has been caring for within the Aerofort will be relocated to my residence near the Palace of the Firmament. The estate is sufficiently large, and the children deserve a home that is neither institutional nor temporary.

Iskander himself mentioned that one **Elizabeth Sasha Constance Eustice**, a young woman of similar circumstances, has been assisting him in their care. I find the arrangement encouraging. Shared responsibility often forges stronger bonds than deliberate mentorship, and a little push might prevent him from following down this tunneled vision I kept for my own life.

If they remain together, they may provide one another the stability neither was given in childhood.

---

Before concluding, I must record a final hypothesis that may be interpreted as philosophical rather than scientific.

During my studies of ancient civilizations, I found endless documentation of stagnation. Empires collapsing beneath superstition, ignorance, and internal contradiction.

Aerolon has experienced none of these impediments.

In only a few centuries we have achieved industrial, alchemical, and atmospheric mastery that other societies required millennia to approach, if they approached it at all. Do not mistake this for nationalist propaganda.

Every experiment I conducted produced results.

My few failures produced usable data.

Progress has never resisted us.

This absence of resistance now strikes me as statistically improbable.

If one were inclined toward theological thinking — a habit I have long dismissed — one might conclude that our civilization exists within a framework where discovery itself behaves as a natural law.

Science here does not struggle against reality.

It unfolds from it.

One might almost call that divine.

I do not claim such certainty. But I leave the thought behind for those who will inherit the work I cannot complete.

The procedure scheduled for tomorrow will conclude my life before the deterioration progresses further.

I do not fear death.

I fear the version of myself that might exist if I delay.

*The voice* argues that my obligations are irrelevant, that Starglass is merely a specimen, that Iskander is expendable, that the city itself deserves to be dismantled piece by piece.

I refuse to allow that temperament the opportunity to act.

I have lived as a man of conviction.

I will conclude as one.

If circumstances permit, I will first attempt the final procedure necessary to free Starglass from her current mechanical prison. Should success prove possible, she will be granted autonomy in whatever form she chooses.

Afterward, my own departure will proceed as planned.

Progress must never be halted.

*— Dr. Morgenstern Alistair Larkin*
*Luminary of the Council*
*Aerolon*
*Year 1719*

## Notes

- This is Larkin's **suicide note** — he planned to end his life the day after writing this
- He brought something back from the Deep that infected his mind with "impulses that advocate violence"
- He planned to **free Starglass first**, then die — unknown if he succeeded with either
- He calls Liz by her full birth name: **Elizabeth Sasha Constance Eustice** — the Larkin name was not yet hers when he wrote this
- Larkin's estate is "near the Palace of the Firmament" — this is where he planned to relocate the orphans
- His observation that Aerolon's progress has "never been resisted" and science "unfolds from reality" there is deeply unsettling given what followed
